Behaviour

Bees are social insects that live and work together in colonies. Each bee has a role that helps the colony survive, and bees communicate with each other in several different ways. They use movement, sounds, touch, food sharing and chemicals called pheromones to pass information to other bees.

One of the most well-known behaviours of honey bees is dancing. When a worker bee finds a good source of food, it can return to the hive and perform a waggle dance. The direction and length of the dance give other bees information about where the food is. The direction shows the direction of the food compared with the sun, while the length of the waggle tells the other bees roughly how far away it is.

Bees also perform a round dance when food is close to the hive. The round dance does not give the same detailed information about direction and distance as the waggle dance, but it tells other bees that food has been found nearby. Other bees can then leave the hive and search for the food source.

Bees also communicate using pheromones. Pheromones are chemical signals that bees release to influence the behaviour of other bees. The queen produces pheromones that help maintain the organisation of the colony and affect the behaviour of worker bees. Worker bees can also release pheromones. For example, when a bee stings an intruder, it can release an alarm pheromone that attracts other bees and encourages them to defend the colony.

Roles

Different bees have different roles in the colony. The queen is the main reproductive female and her most important role is to lay eggs. A healthy queen can lay a large number of eggs, allowing the colony to grow and replace bees that die.

Most of the bees in a colony are worker bees. Worker bees are female and do not normally reproduce. They perform many different jobs during their lives. Young worker bees usually stay inside the hive and clean cells, feed larvae and care for the queen. As they get older, they can produce wax, build honeycomb, receive food from returning foragers and guard the entrance of the hive.

Older worker bees often become foragers. Foragers leave the hive to collect nectar, pollen, water and other resources. They remember the locations of useful flowers and can communicate information about good food sources to other bees through their dances.

Some worker bees act as guard bees. They stay near the entrance of the hive and inspect bees that are trying to enter. They can recognise members of their own colony by their smell and can attack animals or insects that threaten the hive.

Another important role is that of the undertaker bee. These worker bees remove dead bees and other unwanted material from the hive. Removing dead bodies helps keep the colony clean and reduces the risk of disease spreading.

Male bees are called drones. Their main role is to mate with queens from other colonies. Drones do not collect food or raise young, and they do not have a sting. During times when food is scarce, worker bees may prevent drones from being fed or force them out of the colony.

The roles of worker bees are not always fixed. A worker bee can change jobs as it gets older or as the needs of the colony change. For example, if the colony needs more guards, some bees may begin guarding the entrance instead of performing another task. This allows the colony to respond to changes in its environment.

Bee behaviour is therefore based on cooperation between many individuals. Bees communicate where food can be found, warn each other about danger, care for young bees, defend the colony and keep the hive clean. Although each individual bee is small, the combined behaviour of thousands of bees allows the colony to function as a highly organised society.
